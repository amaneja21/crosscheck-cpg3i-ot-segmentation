Two deployment shapes, one topology

Section 5.2's collection method invariance claim isn't only about how traffic is captured, it's about where enforcement and visibility actually live, and in a real deployment those aren't always the same box. Same topology, two different answers about what evidence exists at all.

Inline

Level 3.5 · DMZ · perimeter NGFW or /32 host-routed (Zero Trust Branch)

A plain perimeter NGFW only sees traffic that was already routed through it, in practice the IT/OT boundary and nothing else. Two devices in the same area segment talk directly to each other; the appliance was never in that path, so it never sees it.

Zero Trust Branch changes that by handing every device, PLC, HMI, whatever's on the segment, a /32 host route pointing back at the appliance. That removes the direct path between two devices on the same subnet entirely, everything a device originates goes through the appliance first, including traffic that would otherwise stay inside one area. The appliance becomes the default next hop for every device, not just the perimeter.

Under a plain perimeter NGFW, Clauses 1 and 3 are checkable, Clause 2 typically has nothing to check against. Under a /32 host-routed deployment, Clause 2 becomes checkable from that same single appliance, since it now sees inter-area traffic too. Worth treating these as two distinct inline sub-cases in the dataset, not one.

Passive / SPAN

Level 3 core switch, or per area · e.g. Claroty

Tap or SPAN based sensors sit further down and stream what they see to a cloud analysis platform. This gets visibility everywhere, including the inter-area traffic a perimeter-only inline deployment wouldn't see, but it's visibility only.

The vendor providing it doesn't enforce anything. Any policy for inter-area traffic lives as ACLs on switches and routers from a separate networking vendor, Cisco, Fortinet, whoever operates that layer, configured independently of the visibility platform.

Clause 2 is checkable here too, but the traffic evidence and the policy evidence come from two systems that were never designed to talk to each other, a different failure mode than the /32 host-routed inline case, not a harder or more realistic one. Worth a specific mention in Section 4.2, not just 5.2, since it's a genuinely different flavor of multi-vendor correlation.

How this maps to CPG 3.I

The four clauses from Section 4.3, and which part of the diagram exercises each one.

Clause 1
Boundary

The IT/OT boundary has one real enforcement device, the inline firewall in the DMZ. The planted violation for this clause is the boundary missing entirely, IT traffic reaching Level 3 with nothing stateful in the path, which is a check the inline collection method alone can make with confidence.

Clause 2
Containment

This one depends on deployment shape, and inline isn't uniformly blind here. Under a plain perimeter NGFW, or passive/SPAN with no switch ACLs behind it, nothing is enforcing east-west traffic and the honest answer is no evidence to check. Under a /32 host-routed deployment, or passive/SPAN paired with switch ACLs, the planted violation is a rule broad enough to let a breach in one area's segment reach another's, and now there's real evidence to check it against.

Clause 3
Physical enclave

The SIS enclave is the only asset in this topology that isn't reachable through a normal layered boundary at all, it's bridged by a diode that should only ever carry traffic one way, disinfection status out to the historian, nothing back in. The compliant case is genuinely one-directional traffic on that link; the planted violation is a single packet observed going the wrong way.

This is also where the open question from the paper draft lives, whether a policy-enforced one-way rule could ever stand in for the physical diode here. Until that's resolved, every case built against this enclave should assume hardware enforcement.
Clause 4
Exception

"Where safe and technically feasible" is CPG 3.I's own scope language, not something added for this paper, so the clause itself stays. It doesn't map to a fixed device in this topology though; feasibility is a judgment the reasoning layer applies wherever segmentation turns out to be operationally or safety constrained, not a property of one asset. Left without a dedicated example here on purpose, it gets a concrete test case when the labeled dataset is actually built, rather than forcing a device into the reference topology to justify the clause's presence.

Open questions before this becomes a config

Flag anything here before it gets baked into the simulator's topology definition.

  1. Four process areas, intake, filtration, disinfection, distribution, is the granularity used throughout. Real distribution networks are often split into multiple pressure zones; worth deciding now whether that adds a useful containment test case or just adds boxes.
  2. Level 4 Enterprise IT is drawn only for context and sits outside CPG 3.I's scope entirely. Fine to leave as backdrop, or should it be dropped so the diagram, and the eventual topology config, focuses purely on the OT side.
  3. VLAN numbering here is a placeholder scheme. Worth confirming whether it should mirror something closer to a real customer deployment pattern you've seen through Zero Trust Branch, since that would make the synthetic policies read as more representative.
  4. The SIS enclave's function, chlorine disinfection interlock plus high-high level shutdown, is one plausible example. It becomes the fixed reference case for Section 4.3's data diode clause throughout the rest of the paper, so worth confirming it's the right one before it's load bearing.
